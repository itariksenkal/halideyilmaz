Children are meeting AI earlier and more naturally than most of us did: as a homework helper, inside games, in voice assistants, or in chatbots that talk like a friend. Many parents swing between banning it outright and not getting involved at all. In this post I want to suggest a realistic middle path that needs no technical background. What do age limits actually say? How should AI be used for homework? What should you watch for on privacy? What is the risk of emotional attachment? And how can you talk to your child about all of this?

## Age limits: what the terms of service say

Most AI services set a minimum age in their terms of service. A common arrangement for general-purpose chatbots is that children under 13 cannot open an account, and users aged 13 to 17 need permission from a parent or legal guardian. These limits differ between services and can change over time, so it is worth checking the current terms of every app your child uses.

Age also matters legally. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) sets 16 as the default age at which a child can consent to online services processing their data; member states may lower it, but not below 13. Below that age, parental consent is required.

One uncomfortable fact is worth accepting: age checks often rely on users stating their own birthday, which is easy to get around. An age limit is a starting point, not a safety net.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Try the AI app your child uses yourself first. Going through parental controls, chat history settings and data-sharing options together tends to have a more lasting effect than a ban.</div>

## Homework and learning: helper or shortcut?

AI can genuinely help: re-explaining a topic in simpler words, walking through a maths step or giving feedback on a piece of writing. The trouble starts when a child hands over the thinking entirely.

A few practical distinctions help:

| Healthier use | Riskier use |
|---|---|
| "Can you explain this more simply?" | "Write my homework for me." |
| Asking for feedback on their own draft | Submitting generated text as it is |
| Checking the answer against another source | Accepting the answer without question |
| Following the teacher's rules on AI | Using it without knowing the school's rules |

It is especially important to tell children that AI can state wrong information in a very confident voice. "What is the source?" and "Where else could we check this?" are among the most valuable habits they can build right now.

## Privacy: what is your child sharing?

When children chat with a bot, they may share their name, their school, their friends' names, even problems at home. How that information is stored, how long it is kept and whether it is used to improve models varies from service to service.

In Turkey, the Personal Data Protection Authority (KVKK) published its guide "Generative AI and the Protection of Personal Data (in 15 Questions)" on 24 November 2025. It includes a section on what individuals should watch for in daily life and on measures parents can take when children use these tools. It is a useful official resource for Turkish-speaking families.

Simple rules work well: no full name, address, school name, photos or passwords. "Would you tell this to a stranger?" is a good test.

## Emotional attachment: can a chatbot be a friend?

This is, I think, the least discussed and most important issue. Some apps are explicitly designed as "friends" or "companions": always available, never bored, always agreeable.

A survey published in July 2025 by Common Sense Media, a US non-profit, found that 72% of US teens aged 13 to 17 had used AI companions at least once, and more than half used them regularly. The organisation recommends that no one under 18 use AI companion apps. In September 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) asked seven companies for information as part of a study into how companion-style chatbots affect children and teens.

UNICEF's "Guidance on AI and children", whose third version was published in December 2025, also added AI companions used by children as a new topic.

Signs worth noticing:

- A child pulling away from real friends and family,
- Becoming restless when they cannot talk to the chatbot,
- Sharing personal or emotional problems only with an app.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Explain clearly what a chatbot is: it has no feelings, it does not know your child, it only produces text. Then agree together on which real people your child can talk to when something is hard.</div>

## How to talk with children about AI

A ban may feel easier in the short term, but children will usually meet these tools anyway, at school, through friends or on other devices. What really protects them is the ability to think critically.

- **Start with curiosity.** Ask "Which AI apps do you use, and what do you like about them?" as a conversation, not an interrogation.
- **Try it together.** Ask a question side by side and check the answer together. Catching a wrong answer is a great teaching moment.
- **Match their age.** For young children: "It is a computer program, and sometimes it gets things wrong." For teenagers: deeper questions about data, advertising and design choices.
- **Set the rules together.** Agree on when, for what and without sharing which information the tools will be used.
- **Lead by example.** How adults use these tools is the strongest message children receive.

International bodies point in the same direction. The OECD's Recommendation on children in the digital environment puts the child's best interests first and stresses empowering children and their parents and building their resilience.
