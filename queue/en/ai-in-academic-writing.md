Since generative AI tools became widespread, one of the most common questions in universities has been: "Can I use these for my thesis or paper?" The answer is neither "absolutely not" nor "however you like". Over the past few years publishers, ethics bodies and universities have drawn an increasingly clear line: AI can be an assistant but not an author; if you use it, you disclose it; and the human author is responsible for every sentence of the result. In this post I want to go through that line in more detail, the trap of fabricated references, and why AI detectors should not be trusted blindly.

## The general framework: assistant yes, author no

The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), one of the main reference points for publishing ethics, set out a clear principle in its February 2023 position statement: AI tools cannot be listed as authors of a paper. The reasoning is simple. Authorship carries responsibility, and an AI tool cannot take responsibility for the work, declare conflicts of interest, or manage copyright and licence agreements. COPE also says authors who use AI in writing, producing images or collecting and analysing data should disclose how and which tool was used, and that they remain fully responsible for the whole manuscript, including the parts an AI produced.

The large publishers follow the same line. Springer Nature states that large language models such as ChatGPT do not satisfy its authorship criteria, that their use should be documented in the methods or another suitable section, and it generally does not accept images created with generative AI. Elsevier asks authors to include a separate AI declaration at submission, which also appears in the published article and should name the tool, its purpose and the extent of the author's oversight. According to Elsevier, basic spelling, grammar and punctuation checks do not need to be declared, but use that substantially changes sentence structure, organisation or content should be.

Universities have issued their own guidance too. In Türkiye, for example, the Council of Higher Education (YÖK) published an ethics guide in 2024 on the use of generative AI in research and publication, and many universities have built their own rules on it. Anyone writing a thesis should check the current rules of their own university and department.

## What is usually allowed, and what is not

Rules vary between institutions, but the general picture looks roughly like this:

| Usually acceptable (with disclosure) | Usually not acceptable |
|---|---|
| Improving the language and readability of text you wrote | Listing AI as an author |
| Brainstorming or asking for a draft outline | Presenting AI-generated text as your own original contribution |
| Finding starting points for a literature search (and checking every source yourself) | Using references without verifying them |
| Debugging code or asking for an explanation of analysis steps | Creating or altering experimental or measurement images with AI |
| Using AI as part of the method (described in the methods section) | Uploading a manuscript you are reviewing into an AI tool |

The last row is often overlooked. Publishers such as Elsevier explicitly ask reviewers not to upload a submitted manuscript, or any part of it, into AI tools, because this can breach the authors' confidentiality and proprietary rights. The same logic applies to pasting a student's unpublished thesis into a chatbot.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Keep a short log of every stage where you use AI (which tool, which date, for what purpose). When you need to write a disclosure, or your supervisor asks, these notes make life much easier.</div>

## Fabricated references: the sneakiest trap

One of the best-known problems with large language models is that they can produce information that does not exist but looks entirely realistic. In academic writing, the most dangerous form of this is fabricated references. In a 2023 study by Gravel and colleagues in Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Digital Health, researchers asked ChatGPT 20 medical questions and requested references; of the 59 references they evaluated, 41, about 69%, were fabricated. What is more, the fakes often used names of authors who had genuinely published in the field, relevant-sounding titles and credible journal formats.

Other studies suggest these rates have fallen with newer models, but the problem has not gone away. The rule is simple: never use a source without opening it, reading it and confirming it says what you claim. Check not only that the source exists, but that the specific point you cite is actually in it. Elsevier's policy likewise asks authors to verify every reference, noting that AI can fabricate citations.

## Why AI detectors cannot be relied on

Some institutions run student work through "AI detectors". Understanding their limits matters for students and for the academics who assess them.

One of the clearest signals came from OpenAI itself, the developer of ChatGPT, which withdrew its own AI text classifier in July 2023, citing its low rate of accuracy. By the company's own evaluation, the tool correctly identified only about 26% of AI-written text and wrongly labelled about 9% of human-written text as AI-generated.

More troubling is that the errors do not fall equally on everyone. In a 2023 study published in Patterns, Liang and colleagues at Stanford tested seven detectors on 91 TOEFL essays written by non-native English speakers and on essays by US eighth-grade students. The detectors were almost flawless on the US students' essays but classified more than half of the TOEFL essays as AI-generated, with an average false positive rate above 61%. The researchers linked this to the more limited, predictable word choices of non-native writers.

From my perspective as someone who works on model evaluation, the conclusion is that a detector score is not evidence on its own. Accusing a student on the basis of a detector score alone can lead to serious injustice, especially for students writing in a second language. Draft history, a conversation with the student and questions about the content of the work are far more meaningful evidence.

## A practical checklist

If you are thinking of using AI while writing a thesis or paper, I would suggest this order:

1. **Read the rules:** Check the current AI policy of your university, department and target journal.
2. **Talk to your supervisor:** Agree early on which uses are acceptable.
3. **Do not outsource the thinking:** The research question, choice of methods, interpretation of findings and conclusions should be your own contribution.
4. **Verify every source:** Never use a reference you have not opened and read.
5. **Protect confidentiality:** Do not upload unpublished data, participant information or other people's work into AI tools.
6. **Disclose:** State clearly which tool you used, for what purpose and with what oversight.
