Bias and Fairness in AI: Why Models Discriminate

HY
Halide Yılmaz
HatsonTech · Co-Founder
· 8 min read· Last updated: 9 August 2026

A model has no malice; it simply learns the world you show it. If that world is skewed, the model learns the skew — and then scales it. Bias is therefore as much a definitional problem as a technical one: it depends on what you chose to predict and which data you taught it with.

Where bias comes from

  • Historical bias: the data reflects past decisions. If those decisions were skewed, so is the data.
  • Representation bias: some groups appear rarely in the dataset, and the model performs worse for them.
  • Measurement bias: the target is not what you actually want to measure. If you record "arrests" rather than "crime", the model learns patrol density.
  • Aggregation bias: distinct groups are compressed into one model that fits none of them well.
A crucial distinction: dropping the sensitive attribute does not remove bias. The model can reconstruct it from postcode, name or purchase history.

The proxy-variable trap

Deleting a gender or ethnicity column is not enough, because other variables carry that information indirectly. These are proxies. The real answer is to measure and monitor the sensitive attribute rather than delete it. You cannot correct an inequity you refuse to measure.

Fairness metrics

MetricWhat it demands
Demographic parityEqual rate of positive predictions across groups
Equal opportunityEqual true-positive rate across groups
Equalised oddsEqual true- and false-positive rates
CalibrationThe same score means the same real probability in every group

Why the metrics conflict

These criteria cannot all hold at once. When base rates differ between groups, calibration and equalised odds are mathematically incompatible. This is not an engineering shortcoming but a proven impossibility.

The consequence is that there is no single definition of a "fair model". Which definition you adopt is a value judgement tied to your domain and to who is harmed by which error — and it should be written down explicitly.

What to do in practice

  1. Identify the sensitive groups and measure performance separately for each.
  2. Judge by the worst-performing group, not the aggregate metric.
  3. Close representation gaps with targeted data collection.
  4. Document the fairness definition you chose and why.
  5. Keep monitoring by group in production; when the distribution shifts, fairness shifts with it.

Frequently asked questions

Is the model biased, or the data?

Almost always the data and the problem definition. The model merely amplifies the relationships it is shown.

Is collecting sensitive data a privacy problem?

Fairness auditing is a legitimate purpose, but it requires separate legal assessment, strict purpose limitation and access control. Data gathered for auditing should not be used as a model input.

How do I measure this for small groups?

With confidence intervals. In small samples, group differences are indistinguishable from noise; do not draw conclusions without reporting uncertainty.

Fairness is not a filter bolted on afterwards but a design decision that starts with the problem definition. For related reading, see model interpretability and data quality.
